## The Big Read

# AI Capital Has Entered the Mega-Round Era

Artificial intelligence is no longer simply the fastest-growing category in venture capital. It is becoming the category that defines the entire market.

Investment in U.S. and Canadian startups reached **$392 billion during the first half of 2026**, according to reported Crunchbase data. The total was driven largely by giant late-stage financings rather than by a broad increase in the number of deals. <citation src="2"></citation>

The pattern is becoming clear: investors are concentrating capital around companies with access to computing power, enterprise distribution, proprietary data, and a credible path to large-scale revenue.

That leaves early-stage founders facing a more demanding market. A strong technical idea may open the door, but investors increasingly want evidence of adoption, infrastructure advantages, and an economic model that can survive expensive model training and deployment.

# Top AI News

## Anthropic Raises a Landmark $30 Billion

Anthropic’s reported **$30 billion financing** was identified as the largest funding round among the major February 2026 startup financings covered in the available market data.

The deal reinforced the growing importance of model companies with substantial computing requirements and enterprise demand. It also demonstrated how quickly AI fundraising has moved beyond traditional venture-scale rounds. <citation src="3"></citation>

---

## Open-Weight AI Intensifies the Model Race

Meta released **Glimmer**, an open-weight AI model that users can download and run on their own hardware, while keeping its more powerful **Muse Spark** model available through APIs.

The move reflects a continuing strategic divide in the industry: open distribution can accelerate adoption and developer experimentation, while closed APIs preserve control over infrastructure, pricing, and product access. <citation src="1"></citation>

---

## AI Coding Tools Become Acquisition Targets

SpaceX completed a reported **$60 billion acquisition of Cursor and its parent company, Anysphere**, creating what was described as the largest startup acquisition on record.

The transaction underlines the strategic value of software-development tools that can become embedded in everyday engineering workflows. <citation src="2"></citation>

---

## AI Infrastructure Draws Billions

Cerebras Systems raised **$5.6 billion in its May 2026 public offering**, highlighting investor appetite for companies building chips and infrastructure for AI workloads.

The financing environment increasingly favors the companies supplying the computational foundation of AI, not only those building consumer-facing applications. <citation src="2"></citation>

---

## Physical AI Moves Toward the Factory Floor

Global venture funding in physical AI reached **$47.4 billion across 521 deals during the first half of 2026**, according to reported Crunchbase figures.

The category includes robotics, autonomous machines, industrial systems, and software designed to understand and act in physical environments. <citation src="2"></citation>

# Startup News

## Rillet Reaches Billion-Dollar Valuation

Financial software company Rillet raised a reported **$100 million Series C** and reached a **$1 billion valuation** approximately two years after emerging from stealth.

The round is another example of investor interest in software that automates complex finance and accounting operations for businesses. <citation src="1"></citation>

---

## Castelion Targets Defense Manufacturing

Defense startup Castelion reached a reported **$13 billion valuation** as it works to mass-produce hypersonic missiles.

The company’s valuation reflects a broader investment trend toward defense technology, advanced manufacturing, aerospace, and systems supported by substantial government demand. <citation src="1"></citation>

---

## Startup Funding Hits Record Levels, but Deal Counts Remain Lower

North American venture investment reached **$137.2 billion in the second quarter of 2026**, the second-highest quarterly total on record.

The market is not experiencing an even distribution of capital. A small group of late-stage AI companies accounted for a substantial portion of the total, while the overall number of deals remained below previous highs. <citation src="2"></citation>

## The New Startup Advantage

The strongest startups in the current market tend to share several characteristics:

- They solve expensive enterprise problems.
- They use AI as part of a workflow rather than as a superficial feature.
- They own specialized data, distribution, or infrastructure.
- They can demonstrate revenue efficiency.
- They have a clear reason to exist beyond the availability of venture capital.

# Canada IT Startups

## Canadian Founders Face a Global Capital Market

Canadian information-technology startups are operating in a market where the largest financing rounds are increasingly concentrated in artificial intelligence, infrastructure, robotics, and advanced computing.

For Canadian founders, the opportunity is significant but competitive. Companies that can sell into the United States and other international markets may have a stronger fundraising story than companies relying only on domestic demand.

---

## Toronto Remains a Major AI Corridor

Toronto’s technology ecosystem continues to benefit from deep academic, engineering, and machine-learning talent.

The most attractive Canadian opportunities are likely to emerge where research strength meets commercial execution:

- Enterprise AI
- Financial technology
- Cybersecurity
- Developer tools
- Health technology
- Robotics
- Cloud infrastructure
- Data management

## Montréal’s Research Advantage

Montréal remains an important center for artificial-intelligence research and applied machine learning.

The city’s startup opportunity lies in converting research expertise into products that can serve global customers. The next stage of growth will depend on commercialization, access to computing resources, and the ability to attract experienced operators.

---

## Waterloo Builds for Developers and Industry

The Waterloo region continues to produce companies focused on software engineering, cybersecurity, enterprise applications, and advanced technology.

Its strongest advantage is the connection between technical talent, university research, and practical product development. Startups in the region may benefit from targeting specialized industries where trust and technical depth matter more than consumer visibility.

## What Investors Are Funding

### 1. Compute

AI models require specialized chips, data centers, networking equipment, and energy. This makes infrastructure one of the most capital-intensive parts of the technology economy.

### 2. Agents

Investors are backing software that can perform multi-step tasks rather than simply answer questions.

### 3. Robotics

Physical AI is attracting attention because it connects software intelligence to factories, warehouses, vehicles, and other real-world systems.

### 4. Enterprise Automation

Companies that reduce labor-intensive administrative work remain attractive, particularly in finance, customer support, legal services, and compliance.

### 5. Defense Technology

Advanced aerospace, autonomous systems, and manufacturing platforms are receiving increased attention from both private and public-sector buyers.

# The Editor’s View

## The AI Market Is Splitting in Two

One part of the market is funding enormous infrastructure projects and model companies. The other is building focused software products on top of those systems.

Both markets can succeed, but they face different tests.

Infrastructure companies must prove that their capital requirements can produce durable technical advantages. Application companies must prove that customers will continue paying once AI features become common across competing products.

The winners may not be the companies with the most impressive demonstrations. They may be the companies that quietly become essential to how work gets done.

# Founder’s Notebook

## Five Questions Before Raising Capital

1. Is the product solving an expensive problem?
2. Does the company own a defensible advantage?
3. Can customers measure the return on investment?
4. Does the business work without permanently subsidized computing?
5. Is the company prepared for a slower fundraising market?
